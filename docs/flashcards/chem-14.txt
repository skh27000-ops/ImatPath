chem14-012	Key points: <b>Rate</b>	• Rate = change in concentration / time (mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>). It is the <b>slope</b> of the concentration-time graph and falls with time.	ImatPath Chemistry summary
chem14-013	Key points: <b>Collision theory</b>	• A reaction needs a collision with <b>energy <i>≥ E<sub>a</sub></i></b> and the <b>right orientation</b>.<br>• Raise concentration, pressure or surface area: more collisions.<br>• Raise temperature: more collisions and <b>many more with <i>E ≥ E<sub>a</sub></i></b>.<br>• <b>Catalyst</b>: lower <i>E<sub>a</sub></i>, same <i>Δ H</i>, same equilibrium, not used up.	ImatPath Chemistry summary
chem14-014	Key points: <b>Rate equation</b>	• rate = <i>k[A]<sup>m</sup></i>. Order from experiment. Double <i>[A]</i>: rate × <i>2<sup>m</sup></i>.<br>• First order: constant <b>half-life</b>. Fraction left = <i>(1/2)<sup>n</sup></i>.	ImatPath Chemistry summary
chem14-015	Key points: <b>Enzymes</b>	• Biological catalysts, specific active site, optimum temperature and pH, <b>denatured</b> when hot.	ImatPath Chemistry summary
chem14-004	Initial rates for A + B → products: (1) [A] = 0.10, [B] = 0.10, rate = <i>2.0 × 10<sup>-3</sup></i>. (2) [A] = 0.20, [B] = 0.10, rate = <i>8.0 × 10<sup>-3</sup></i>. (3) [A] = 0.10, [B] = 0.30, rate = <i>6.0 × 10<sup>-3</sup></i> (mol/L, mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>). What is the rate when [A] = 0.30 and [B] = 0.20?<br><br><br>A) 0.012 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup><br>B) 0.036 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup><br>C) 0.12 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup><br>D) 0.0072 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup><br>E) 0.0108 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>	<b>B) 0.036 mol L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup></b><br><br>Doubling [A] quadruples the rate (1→2): second order in A. Tripling [B] triples the rate (1→3): first order in B. Rate <i>= k[A]<sup>2</sup>[B]</i>. From (1): <i>k = 2.0 × 10<sup>-3</sup>/(0.10<sup>2</sup> × 0.10) = 2.0</i>. Then rate <i>= 2.0 × 0.30<sup>2</sup> × 0.20 = 0.036</i>.	ImatPath Chemistry practice

chem14-008	The forward reaction has <i>E<sub>a</sub> = 80</i> kJ/mol and <i>Δ H = -30</i> kJ/mol. A catalyst lowers the forward activation energy by 25 kJ/mol. What is the activation energy of the <b>reverse</b> reaction <b>with</b> the catalyst?<br><br><br>A) 110 kJ/mol<br>B) 55 kJ/mol<br>C) 135 kJ/mol<br>D) 30 kJ/mol<br>E) 85 kJ/mol	<b>E) 85 kJ/mol</b><br><br>Without catalyst: <i>E<sub>a</sub>(reverse) = E<sub>a</sub>(forward) - Δ H = 80 + 30 = 110</i> kJ/mol. The catalyst lowers both barriers by the same amount (25 kJ/mol): <i>110 - 25 = 85</i> kJ/mol. <i>Δ H</i> is unchanged.	ImatPath Chemistry practice
chem14-010	Why does a 10 °C rise in temperature roughly double the rate of many reactions?<br><br><br>A) The particles collide twice as often<br>B) The activation energy is halved<br>C) The reaction becomes more exothermic<br>D) A much larger fraction of the collisions has energy at or above the activation energy<br>E) The concentration of the reactants doubles	<b>D) A much larger fraction of the collisions has energy at or above the activation energy</b><br><br>The collision frequency rises only a few percent. The big effect is on the <b>fraction</b> of particles with <i>E ≥ E<sub>a</sub></i>, which grows rapidly with temperature. The activation energy and <i>Δ H</i> do not change.	ImatPath Chemistry practice
