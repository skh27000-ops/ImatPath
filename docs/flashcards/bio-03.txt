bio03-024	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Attachment</b>	surface proteins bind the receptor on the host cell.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-025	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Entry</b>	the genome (or the whole particle) enters the cell.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-026	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Replication</b>	the host machinery copies the viral nucleic acid and makes viral proteins.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-027	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Assembly</b>	new capsids form and fill with genomes.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-028	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Release</b>	new viruses leave, by <b>bursting</b> the cell (lysis) or by <b>budding</b> through the membrane.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-029	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Lytic cycle</b>	the phage DNA takes over the cell, many new phages are made, and the cell <b>bursts (lyses)</b> to release them. The infection is fast and destroys the host.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-030	<small>How viruses multiply</small><br><b>Lysogenic cycle</b>	the phage DNA is <b>inserted into the bacterial chromosome</b> (it is then called a <b>prophage</b>) and is copied every time the bacterium divides. Nothing visible happens until a stress signal triggers the lytic cycle.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-031	<small>Viruses and health</small><br><b>Vaccination</b>	teaches the immune system to recognise the virus in advance.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-032	<small>Viruses and health</small><br><b>Antivirals</b>	drugs that block a specific viral step (for example, stopping reverse transcription).	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-033	<small>Answers & explanations</small><br><b>Obligate intracellular parasite</b>	reproduces only in a host cell.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-034	<small>Answers & explanations</small><br><b>Retrovirus</b>	RNA → DNA with <b>reverse transcriptase</b>; infects helper T cells.	ImatPath Biology key idea
bio03-009	Key points: <b>Structure</b>	• Nucleic acid (DNA <b>or</b> RNA) inside a protein <b>capsid</b>. Some have an <b>envelope</b>.<br>• Not a cell: no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, no metabolism.<br>• <b>Obligate intracellular parasite</b>: reproduces only in a host cell.	ImatPath Biology summary
bio03-010	Key points: <b>Replication</b>	• Attach, enter, replicate, assemble, release (lysis or budding).<br>• Phage: <b>lytic</b> (bursts the cell) or <b>lysogenic</b> (prophage in the chromosome).<br>• <b>Retrovirus</b> (HIV): RNA → DNA with <b>reverse transcriptase</b>; infects helper T cells.	ImatPath Biology summary
bio03-011	Key points: <b>Health</b>	• Vaccines and antivirals work. <b>Antibiotics do not</b>.<br>• Flu changes quickly because RNA copying is error-prone.	ImatPath Biology summary
bio03-001	Which order correctly describes the early steps of HIV replication in a helper T cell?<br><br><br>A) Viral DNA is copied into RNA, then reverse transcriptase makes protein<br>B) RNA is copied into DNA by reverse transcriptase, the DNA is integrated into the host chromosome, then viral RNA and proteins are made<br>C) The host makes viral RNA, then the RNA is integrated into the chromosome<br>D) Viral RNA is translated first, then copied into DNA by the host DNA polymerase<br>E) Viral DNA is integrated, then copied into RNA by reverse transcriptase	<b>B) RNA is copied into DNA by reverse transcriptase, the DNA is integrated into the host chromosome, then viral RNA and proteins are made</b><br><br>HIV is a retrovirus with an RNA genome. Reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy, which integrates into the host DNA (as a provirus). The host machinery then transcribes it to make new viral RNA and proteins.	ImatPath Biology practice
bio03-003	A patient with a viral infection is given antibiotics, but they do not help. Why?<br><br><br>A) Antibiotics target bacterial structures, such as the cell wall or 70S ribosomes, that viruses do not have<br>B) Viruses are too small to be reached by the antibiotic<br>C) Viruses have a thicker cell wall than bacteria<br>D) Antibiotics work only against eukaryotic cells<br>E) Viruses destroy antibiotics with enzymes	<b>A) Antibiotics target bacterial structures, such as the cell wall or 70S ribosomes, that viruses do not have</b><br><br>Antibiotics block processes that are specific to bacteria. A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes and no metabolism of its own, so there is nothing for the antibiotic to attack.	ImatPath Biology practice
bio03-005	A single phage infects one bacterium and 100 new phages are released. Each of those infects a different bacterium and again releases 100 phages. How many phages are released after three rounds in total (counting only the last round)?<br><br><br>A) 300<br>B) 10<sup>3</sup><br>C) 10<sup>5</sup><br>D) 10<sup>9</sup><br>E) 10<sup>6</sup>	<b>E) 10<sup>6</sup></b><br><br>Round 1: 100 phages. Round 2: <i>100 × 100 = 10<sup>4</sup></i>. Round 3: <i>10<sup>4</sup> × 100 = 10<sup>6</sup></i>. Growth is exponential.	ImatPath Biology practice
bio03-007	The nucleic acid of two viruses is analysed. Virus X: A 24%, G 26%, C 26%, T 24%. Virus Y: A 30%, G 28%, C 22%, U 20%. What can you conclude?<br><br><br>A) X has single-stranded RNA; Y has double-stranded DNA<br>B) Both have double-stranded DNA<br>C) Both have single-stranded RNA<br>D) X has double-stranded DNA; Y has single-stranded RNA<br>E) X has single-stranded DNA; Y has double-stranded RNA	<b>D) X has double-stranded DNA; Y has single-stranded RNA</b><br><br>In double-stranded DNA A = T and G = C (24 = 24, 26 = 26), so X is dsDNA. Y contains uracil, so it is RNA, and A ≠ U and G ≠ C suggests it is single-stranded.	ImatPath Biology practice
