bio04-001	What are the 4 main jobs of the plasma membrane?	1) <b>Separates</b> the cell from its surroundings; 2) <b>controls traffic</b> (selective permeability); 3) <b>receives signals</b>; 4) <b>holds the cell together</b> (links to neighbours, cytoskeleton, matrix).	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-002	What does <b>selective permeability</b> mean?	The membrane lets some substances cross easily, others slowly and others not at all.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-003	What 3 kinds of molecules make up a membrane?	<b>Lipids</b> (mostly phospholipids, plus cholesterol), <b>proteins</b> (most of the functions) and <b>carbohydrates</b> (short sugar chains on the outer face).	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-004	How thick is the plasma membrane?	About <b>7 nm</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-005	Why is a phospholipid called <b>amphipathic</b>?	It has a <b>hydrophilic head</b> (phosphate group) and two <b>hydrophobic tails</b> (fatty acids): both a water-loving and a water-fearing region.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-006	Why do phospholipids form a <b>bilayer</b> in water?	The heads face the water on both sides and the tails hide together in the middle, away from water. The bilayer closes into a sealed sac because free edges would expose the tails.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-007	What holds the molecules of a membrane together?	Weak <b>hydrophobic interactions</b>, not covalent bonds. That is why the lipids can slide past each other.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-008	State the <b>fluid mosaic model</b>.	The membrane is a <b>fluid</b> phospholipid bilayer with <b>proteins</b> embedded in it like a mosaic, plus cholesterol and sugar chains.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-009	Integral vs peripheral proteins?	<b>Integral</b>: go into the hydrophobic core (most are transmembrane). <b>Peripheral</b>: not in the bilayer, loosely attached to the surface (often to an integral protein).	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-010	What do the parts of a transmembrane protein inside the bilayer look like?	Stretches of about 20 to 30 <b>nonpolar amino acids</b>, usually coiled into an <b>α helix</b>. The parts outside are hydrophilic.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-011	Glycoprotein vs glycolipid?	Both carry a short branched sugar chain (under about 15 sugars) on the <b>outer</b> face. <b>Glycoprotein</b>: chain on a protein. <b>Glycolipid</b>: chain on a lipid.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-012	What is the role of the membrane carbohydrates?	<b>Cell-cell recognition</b>: they are identity tags. Example: the A, B, AB and O blood groups differ in the sugars on red blood cells.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-013	How fast do lipids move sideways, and how often do they flip-flop?	Neighbouring lipids swap places about <b>10<sup>7</sup> times per second</b> (about 2 µm in 1 s). A flip-flop between layers is <b>very rare</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-014	What did the mouse-human cell fusion experiment show?	The labelled surface proteins of the two cells mixed over the hybrid cell within about an hour: membrane proteins can <b>move sideways</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-015	Why do some membrane proteins not move?	They are anchored to the <b>cytoskeleton</b> inside or to the <b>extracellular matrix</b> outside.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-016	Why must a membrane be <b>fluid</b>?	If too stiff, proteins cannot move and the membrane leaks; if too runny, it cannot support its proteins. Normal fluidity is about that of salad oil.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-017	Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acid tails: effect on fluidity?	<b>Saturated</b>: straight, pack tightly, membrane stiffer. <b>Unsaturated</b>: kinked at the double bond, cannot pack tightly, membrane stays <b>fluid</b> at lower temperatures.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-018	Why is cholesterol called a <b>fluidity buffer</b>?	At warm temperatures (37 °C) it restrains the lipids and makes the membrane <b>less fluid</b>; at low temperatures it prevents tight packing so the membrane <b>stays fluid</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-019	Plants have little cholesterol. What buffers their membrane fluidity?	Related <b>steroid lipids</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-020	How do cold-water fish and winter wheat keep their membranes fluid?	By a high proportion of <b>unsaturated</b> fatty acids (wheat raises it in autumn). Heat-loving bacteria use unusual lipids to avoid being too fluid.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-021	What is membrane <b>sidedness</b>, and why are sugars always on the outside?	The two faces differ. Sugars are added to the <b>lumen side</b> in the ER and Golgi; when a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane its inside becomes the <b>outside of the cell</b>.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-022	Name the 6 functions of membrane proteins.	Transport, enzymatic activity, signal transduction (receptors), cell-cell recognition, intercellular joining, attachment to the cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-023	Why does HIV need CCR5 as well as CD4?	CD4 is the main receptor but CCR5 is a <b>co-receptor</b> HIV must also bind to enter most cells. People without CCR5 resist infection; the drug maraviroc masks it.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-024	Which substances cross the lipid bilayer <b>freely</b>?	<b>Small nonpolar</b> molecules and lipid-soluble ones: O<sub>2</sub>, CO<sub>2</sub>, N<sub>2</sub>, steroid hormones, fatty acids.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-025	Why is water a "special case" for the lipid bilayer?	Water is tiny but <b>polar</b>, so it crosses a bare bilayer only <b>slowly</b>. Cells that move lots of water add <b>aquaporins</b>.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-026	Why can ions not cross a bilayer on their own?	They are charged and surrounded by a shell of water, so they cannot enter the hydrophobic core.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-027	Rank these by how easily they cross a bare lipid bilayer: glucose, O<sub>2</sub>, Na<sup>+</sup>, water.	O<sub>2</sub> (freely) &gt; water (slowly) &gt; glucose (very slowly) &gt; Na<sup>+</sup> (blocked).	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-028	Channel proteins vs carrier proteins?	<b>Channel</b>: a hydrophilic tunnel, very fast, passive only. <b>Carrier</b>: binds the substance and <b>changes shape</b>, slower, used for passive <b>and</b> active transport.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-029	What is an aquaporin and how fast is it?	A <b>water channel</b>; up to about <b>3 billion</b> water molecules per second pass in single file.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-030	What does it mean that transport proteins are <b>specific</b>?	Each moves only one substance or a small group. The red blood cell glucose transporter moves glucose about 50 000 times faster than it crosses alone, and even rejects fructose.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-031	Why can channel proteins <b>not</b> perform active transport?	An open channel only lets substances flow <b>down</b> their gradient. Going against it needs a carrier that uses energy.	ImatPath Biology permeability
bio04-032	Define <b>passive transport</b>.	Movement of a substance across a membrane <b>with no energy from the cell</b>: diffusion, osmosis and facilitated diffusion.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-033	State the rule of <b>diffusion</b>.	A substance moves from where it is <b>more concentrated</b> to where it is <b>less concentrated</b> (down its concentration gradient), until a dynamic equilibrium is reached.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-034	What does <b>dynamic equilibrium</b> mean?	Molecules keep crossing, but at <b>equal rates in both directions</b>, so there is no net movement.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-035	Name 5 factors that make diffusion faster.	Steep concentration gradient, higher temperature, large surface area, short distance, small molecules.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-036	Why does a respiring cell keep taking in oxygen by diffusion?	Respiration uses up O<sub>2</sub> inside, so the concentration gradient into the cell is maintained.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-037	Define <b>osmosis</b>.	The diffusion of <b>water</b> across a selectively permeable membrane, from the side with <b>less solute</b> to the side with <b>more solute</b>.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-038	Why does water move toward the side with more solute?	Water molecules cluster around solute particles, so that side has <b>less free water</b>; water diffuses toward the side with less free water.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-039	Define <b>tonicity</b>.	The ability of a surrounding solution to make a cell gain or lose water. It depends on the concentration of solutes that <b>cannot cross the membrane</b>, compared with inside the cell.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-040	Hypotonic, isotonic, hypertonic: what do they describe and what happens to the water?	They describe the <b>solution</b>. <b>Hypotonic</b>: less solute, water goes in. <b>Isotonic</b>: equal, no net movement. <b>Hypertonic</b>: more solute, water goes out.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-041	What happens to an animal cell in hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic solutions?	Hypotonic: swells and may <b>burst (lysis)</b>. Isotonic: stays normal. Hypertonic: <b>shrivels (crenation)</b>.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-042	What happens to a plant cell in hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic solutions?	Hypotonic: <b>turgid</b> (healthy). Isotonic: <b>flaccid</b> (plant wilts). Hypertonic: <b>plasmolysis</b> (membrane pulls away from the wall).	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-043	What is <b>turgor pressure</b>?	The back pressure of the cell wall against a cell that is taking in water by osmosis. It stops further uptake and keeps non-woody plants upright.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-044	How does <i>Paramecium</i> survive in hypotonic pond water?	Its <b>contractile vacuole</b> pumps out the water that keeps entering by osmosis, like a bilge pump.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-045	Why is 0.1 M NaCl about as concentrated (in particles) as 0.2 M glucose?	NaCl splits into <b>two ions</b>; glucose does not split. Osmosis depends on the <b>number of particles</b>.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-046	What is <b>facilitated diffusion</b>?	Passive movement of polar molecules or ions <b>down their gradient through a transport protein</b> (channel or carrier). No ATP.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-047	What are <b>gated ion channels</b>?	Ion channels that open or close in response to a stimulus: an electrical change (voltage) or a chemical binding to the channel.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-048	Why do kidney cells have many aquaporins?	To take back water from the urine; without it you would lose about <b>180 L</b> of urine a day.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-049	Define <b>active transport</b>.	Movement of a solute <b>against its concentration gradient</b>, which needs energy (usually <b>ATP</b>). It always uses <b>carrier proteins</b> (pumps).	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-050	Facilitated diffusion vs active transport: 2 differences?	Facilitated: <b>no energy</b>, <b>down</b> the gradient, channels or carriers. Active: <b>ATP</b>, <b>against</b> the gradient, carriers only.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-051	What does the sodium-potassium pump do in each cycle?	Uses <b>1 ATP</b> to move <b>3 Na<sup>+</sup> out</b> and <b>2 K<sup>+</sup> in</b>, both against their gradients.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-052	Give the main steps of the Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> pump.	1) 3 Na<sup>+</sup> bind inside; 2) ATP phosphorylates the pump; 3) shape change releases Na<sup>+</sup> outside; 4) 2 K<sup>+</sup> bind outside; 5) phosphate leaves, pump returns to shape; 6) K<sup>+</sup> released inside.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-053	Normally, where is Na<sup>+</sup> high and where is K<sup>+</sup> high in an animal cell?	<b>Na<sup>+</sup> is high outside</b> and low inside; <b>K<sup>+</sup> is high inside</b> and low outside.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-054	What is the <b>membrane potential</b>?	The voltage across the membrane. The inside is <b>negative</b> compared with the outside, between about <b>−50 and −200 mV</b>.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-055	What is an <b>electrogenic pump</b>?	A pump that creates a voltage across the membrane. The Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> pump (3 out, 2 in) moves one net positive charge out per cycle; plants, fungi and bacteria use a <b>proton pump</b>.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-056	What is the <b>electrochemical gradient</b>?	The combined effect of the <b>concentration gradient</b> (chemical force) and the <b>membrane potential</b> (electrical force) on an ion. It decides the true direction of passive ion movement.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-057	What is <b>cotransport</b>?	One transport protein couples the <b>downhill</b> movement of one substance to the <b>uphill</b> movement of another.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-058	How do plant cells take up sucrose?	A proton pump uses ATP to build an H<sup>+</sup> gradient; an <b>H<sup>+</sup>/sucrose cotransporter</b> lets H<sup>+</sup> flow back in and brings sucrose in with it.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-059	Why is cotransport called active transport if the cotransporter does not use ATP?	The gradient that drives it was built by <b>ATP-powered pumps</b>, so energy is used indirectly.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-060	How does oral rehydration therapy for diarrhoea work?	A drink with salt and glucose uses the intestinal <b>sodium-glucose cotransporter</b> to bring sodium (and glucose) into the blood.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-061	How do large molecules such as proteins cross the plasma membrane?	In <b>bulk</b>, packed in <b>vesicles</b> (exocytosis and endocytosis). This needs energy.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-062	Describe <b>exocytosis</b> with examples.	A vesicle (usually from the Golgi) moves along microtubules and <b>fuses</b> with the plasma membrane, releasing its contents outside. Examples: insulin, neurotransmitters, cell wall material in plants.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-063	Name the 3 types of endocytosis.	<b>Phagocytosis</b> (cell eating), <b>pinocytosis</b> (cell drinking) and <b>receptor-mediated endocytosis</b>.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-064	Describe <b>phagocytosis</b>.	The cell wraps <b>pseudopodia</b> around a large particle and encloses it in a <b>food vacuole</b>, which fuses with a lysosome and is digested (amoeba, white blood cells).	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-065	What is <b>pinocytosis</b>?	The cell continually takes in small droplets of fluid in tiny vesicles. It is <b>not specific</b>: everything dissolved comes in.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-066	What makes <b>receptor-mediated endocytosis</b> special?	<b>Receptors</b> bind one specific substance and cluster in a <b>coated pit</b>, which pinches off as a vesicle. The cell can collect large amounts even if the substance is scarce outside.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-067	What goes wrong in familial hypercholesterolaemia?	The <b>LDL receptors</b> are defective or missing, so LDL cholesterol cannot enter cells by endocytosis. Cholesterol builds up in the blood and causes early atherosclerosis.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-068	How is the plasma membrane kept constant in a cell that is not growing?	<b>Exocytosis adds</b> membrane and <b>endocytosis removes</b> it; the two balance. A growing cell adds more than it removes.	ImatPath Biology bulk
bio04-069	Name the main factors that speed up diffusion across a membrane.	Steep <b>concentration gradient</b>, higher <b>temperature</b>, <b>small</b> molecules, <b>lipid-soluble</b> (nonpolar) substances, large <b>surface area</b>, thin membrane / <b>short distance</b>, less dense medium.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-070	Osmolarity vs tonicity?	<b>Osmolarity</b>: total concentration of solute <b>particles</b> in a solution. <b>Tonicity</b>: the effect of a solution on cell volume; it follows osmolarity but counts only solutes that <b>cannot cross</b> the membrane.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-071	Define <b>osmotic pressure</b>.	The pressure that would have to be applied to the more concentrated solution to <b>stop water from entering</b> it. In plant cells osmosis stops when turgor pressure pushes back enough.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-072	Define <b>water potential</b> (Ψ). Unit? Value for pure water?	The potential energy of water compared with pure water. Unit: <b>megapascal (MPa)</b>. <b>Pure water = 0</b>.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-073	Write the water potential equation and the sign of each part.	<b>Ψ = Ψ<sub>s</sub> + Ψ<sub>p</sub></b>. Ψ<sub>s</sub> (solute potential) is <b>zero or negative</b>; Ψ<sub>p</sub> (pressure/turgor) is <b>positive</b> in a walled cell.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-074	In which direction does water move, in terms of water potential?	From <b>higher</b> Ψ (less negative) to <b>lower</b> Ψ (more negative).	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-075	Formula for solute potential? Compute it for 0.1 M sucrose at 25 °C.	<b>Ψ<sub>s</sub> = −iMRT</b> (R = 0.00831 L·MPa/mol·K, T in kelvin). Sucrose: i = 1 → −1 × 0.1 × 0.00831 × 298 ≈ <b>−0.25 MPa</b> (0.1 M NaCl, i ≈ 2, gives −0.50 MPa).	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-076	A flaccid plant cell (Ψ<sub>s</sub> = −0.7 MPa, Ψ<sub>p</sub> = 0) is put in pure water. What happens?	Ψ = −0.7 &lt; 0, so water <b>enters</b>. Turgor rises until Ψ<sub>p</sub> = +0.7 MPa, then Ψ = 0 (same as outside): the cell is <b>turgid</b> and net movement stops.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-077	What is <b>saturation</b> in facilitated diffusion?	Carriers are limited in number. When <b>all are busy</b> the rate reaches a <b>maximum</b>: more substance no longer speeds transport. Simple diffusion has no such limit.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-078	Speed of channels vs carriers?	Channels: tens of millions of molecules per second. Carriers: about <b>1 000 to 1 000 000</b> per second (they must change shape).	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-079	Why does glucose appear in the urine when blood glucose is too high?	The kidney <b>glucose carriers are saturated</b>: they cannot take back all the filtered glucose.	ImatPath Biology passive
bio04-080	Uniporter, symporter, antiporter: define each.	<b>Uniporter</b>: one substance. <b>Symporter</b>: two substances in the <b>same</b> direction. <b>Antiporter</b>: two substances in <b>opposite</b> directions (e.g. the Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> pump).	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-081	Primary vs secondary active transport?	<b>Primary</b>: uses <b>ATP directly</b> (Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup>, Ca<sup>2+</sup> and H<sup>+</sup> ATPases). <b>Secondary</b> (cotransport): uses the <b>gradient</b> made by a primary pump.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-082	Why is K<sup>+</sup> a special case for the electrochemical gradient?	Its <b>concentration gradient</b> pushes K<sup>+</sup> <b>out</b>, but the <b>negative inside</b> pulls it <b>in</b>. For Na<sup>+</sup>, both forces push in.	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-083	Which cell spends most of its energy on the Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> pump?	The <b>red blood cell</b> (the pump is vital to maintain the Na<sup>+</sup> and K<sup>+</sup> imbalance).	ImatPath Biology active
bio04-084	What is the <b>extracellular matrix</b> made of?	Mostly <b>collagen</b> woven with <b>proteoglycans</b>; <b>fibronectin</b> links collagen to <b>integrins</b> in the membrane, which connect to the cytoskeleton.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-085	What does a <b>tight junction</b> do, and which proteins build it?	<b>Seals</b> neighbouring animal cells so nothing leaks between them (claudins, occludins). In the gut, bladder and skin epithelia.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-086	What is a <b>desmosome</b>?	A <b>spot-weld</b> junction: <b>cadherins</b> link two cells and attach to <b>intermediate filaments</b>. Holds cells together in tissues under stretch (skin, heart).	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-087	What is a <b>gap junction</b>?	A pore made of two aligned <b>connexons</b> (6 connexins each) that lets <b>ions and small molecules</b> pass between cells. In the heart it spreads the electrical signal.	ImatPath Biology structure
bio04-088	What do plants use instead of gap junctions and desmosomes?	<b>Plasmodesmata</b>: channels through the cell walls joining the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.	ImatPath Biology structure
